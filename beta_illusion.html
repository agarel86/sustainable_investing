The Beta Illusion

How a high-beta stock picker can look like a genius in a bull market — and why Jensen's alpha exposes the truth.

01 — The setup

Two funds, one market

Imagine two funds investing over three years (Jan 2023 – Dec 2025). The first is a plain S&P 500 index fund — it buys the market and holds it. The second is a "stock picker" that claims active skill, but its secret is simple: it only selects stocks with a beta of approximately 2.

A beta of 2 means every 1% move in the S&P 500 translates into roughly a 2% move in the portfolio. In a rising market, this is a turbocharger. In a falling market, it's an anchor. The manager doesn't need to pick winners — just high-beta names — and the bull market does the rest.

Fund A — Index replicator

  • Passive S&P 500 tracker
  • Broad diversification
  • Market-level risk (β ≈ 1)
  • Low fees, no pretence of skill

Fund B — "Stock picker"

  • Selects only high-beta names
  • Concentrated, volatile portfolio
  • Amplified market risk (β ≈ 2)
  • Higher fees, claims active alpha

We simulate monthly returns using realistic parameters: a 10% annualised expected return and 16% annualised volatility for the S&P 500, a 4% risk-free rate, and some idiosyncratic noise in the high-beta fund. No alpha is embedded in the simulation — all outperformance comes purely from amplified market exposure.

02 — The illusion

Cumulative returns: the seductive picture

At first glance, the high-beta fund looks like a clear winner. It substantially outperforms the index over three years, and an investor focused only on total returns might conclude the manager has genuine skill.

The trap
Raw cumulative returns are the most misleading performance metric in finance. They reward risk-taking with no adjustment for how much volatility was endured to earn those returns.
03 — The numbers

Risk and return side by side

Once we compute annualised volatility and Sharpe ratios, the picture changes. The high-beta fund's extra return is accompanied by proportionally higher risk — the Sharpe ratios are nearly identical.

Key insight
Nearly identical Sharpe ratios tell us the "stock picker" earned no additional return per unit of risk. The manager simply took a bigger bet on the same market factor.
04 — The proof

Jensen's alpha: the definitive test

The Sharpe ratio hints at the truth. Jensen's alpha proves it. We run the classic CAPM regression, regressing the high-beta fund's excess returns against the S&P 500's excess returns:

Rhigh-beta − Rf  =  α  +  β × (RS&P500 − Rf)  +  ε

If the manager has genuine stock-picking skill, α (alpha) should be significantly positive — representing returns that can't be explained by market exposure alone. If not, α should be near zero, meaning all outperformance is just amplified beta.

The tight fit (high R²) confirms the fund's returns are almost entirely explained by its market exposure. The near-zero intercept confirms there is no alpha.

The verdict
05 — Takeaways

What this means for investors

1. High returns ≠ high skill

Any fund that concentrates in high-beta stocks will outperform in a rising market. This is arithmetic, not insight. The correct question is never "how much did you return?" but "how much did you return per unit of risk?"

2. The Sharpe ratio is a first filter

If a fund's Sharpe ratio is no better than the benchmark's, the manager likely isn't adding value — just amplifying exposure. It's a quick sanity check before any deeper analysis.

3. Jensen's alpha is the definitive decomposition

By regressing a fund's excess returns against the market, you cleanly separate the two components of performance: beta (systematic risk exposure) and alpha (residual skill). A beta of 2 with zero alpha means the fund is simply a leveraged index — not a skilled stock picker.

4. Beware the narrative

Fund managers who outperform in bull markets are celebrated in the media and in marketing materials. But performance attribution — using tools like the Jensen regression — is what separates genuine skill from disguised risk-taking. Always look under the hood.
